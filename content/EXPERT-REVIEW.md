# Content review checklist

Every course was revised in v3 for an international audience, with real-world cases. Before publishing a course,
ask a subject expert to confirm the real-case facts and regulatory statements listed for it below. The writers
used only widely reported facts and rounded figures, but these must be verified.

## Advanced Leadership (`advanced-leadership`)

Course file: `content/courses/advanced-leadership.json`. The story world (Orvanta, a consumer goods group based in Dubai, and its cast) is fictional. Real names appear only in the "Real case" cards and in quiz question al-s1-q2.

#### Real cases used (facts relied on)

##### s1: Leading yourself
1. **Johnson & Johnson, Tylenol recall (1982).** In autumn 1982 seven people in the Chicago area died after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules laced with cyanide after the product reached the shelves. J&J did not cause the tampering. It warned the public nationwide, stopped advertising and recalled about 31 million bottles across the US, at a reported cost of about USD 100 million. Its leaders cited the company credo, which puts the people who use its products first. It relaunched Tylenol in tamper-resistant packaging within weeks (November 1982), and the brand recovered much of its market share. Also tested in quiz question **al-s1-q2**.
2. **BP, Deepwater Horizon (2010).** On 20 April 2010 an explosion on the Deepwater Horizon rig (Gulf of Mexico) killed 11 workers and caused the largest marine oil spill in US history; BP was the well's operator. Around 30 May 2010 CEO Tony Hayward told reporters "I'd like my life back"; he apologised soon afterwards. On 27 July 2010 BP announced that he would step down as CEO (Bob Dudley took over on 1 October 2010).

##### s2: Strategy and decisions
3. **Kodak.** In 1975 Kodak engineer Steve Sasson built one of the first digital cameras. Kodak held many digital-imaging patents and sold digital products, but most of its profit long came from film, paper and processing. Eastman Kodak filed for Chapter 11 in January 2012 and emerged in 2013 as a smaller company focused on commercial imaging. (The lesson is framed as a teaching point about choosing what to stop, not as a finding about Kodak management.)
4. **Royal Dutch Shell scenario planning (1970s).** A planning group led by Pierre Wack developed scenarios in the early 1970s, including a supply cut and sharp oil-price rise. The October 1973 embargo and the price rises of 1973–74 roughly quadrupled crude prices. Shell is "widely credited" with adjusting faster than many rivals (worded as reputation, not as a measured fact).

##### s3: Teams
5. **Google, Project Aristotle.** Began 2012; studied more than 180 teams; findings shared publicly in 2015 (re:Work). Psychological safety was the most important of five dynamics; the others were dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, and impact. Who was on the team mattered less than how it worked together.
6. **United Airlines Flight 173 (28 December 1978).** Approaching Portland, Oregon, a landing-gear warning; the aircraft held for about an hour while the crew worked the problem, ran out of fuel and crashed short of the airport; 10 people died. The NTSB found the captain failed to monitor fuel and the other crew members did not successfully communicate their concern; it recommended training in cockpit resource management / assertiveness, which became Crew Resource Management.

##### s4: Change and influence
7. **NHS National Programme for IT (England).** Launched 2002; aim of an electronic care record for every patient through large national supplier contracts; criticised for weak engagement of clinicians and local trusts; dismantling announced September 2011. The Public Accounts Committee (2013) estimated cost at about GBP 9.8bn (rounded to "about GBP 10 billion") and described it as one of the worst and most expensive contracting failures in the public sector (paraphrased, not quoted).
8. **Ford, "One Ford" under Alan Mulally.** Mulally became CEO in September 2006; Ford's 2006 loss was a record of about USD 12.7bn ("more than USD 12 billion"); in late 2006 Ford borrowed about USD 23.6bn, pledging most assets including the blue-oval logo. Weekly Business Plan Review with green/yellow/red ratings; when an executive (Mark Fields, not named in the card) first showed red, Mulally applauded. In 2009 Ford was the only one of the Detroit Three that did not go through bankruptcy or take a government bailout. **Please confirm wording:** Ford did take US Department of Energy advanced-technology vehicle loans in 2009; the card says "avoided bankruptcy and a government bailout", which matches common reporting but an expert may prefer "did not take TARP bailout funds".

##### s5: Culture, ethics, succession
9. **Wells Fargo sales practices.** September 2016: CFPB, OCC and the Los Angeles City Attorney fines totalling about USD 185m over employees opening as many as about 2 million possibly unauthorised accounts; the bank said about 5,300 employees had been fired for such conduct over several years. CEO John Stumpf resigned in October 2016 (not named in the card). A 2017 review raised the estimate to about 3.5 million accounts. February 2020: USD 3bn settlement with the DOJ and SEC, in which the bank acknowledged that onerous/unrealistic sales goals and management pressure drove the conduct.
10. **Apple succession (Jobs to Cook), with Disney as contrast.** Cook joined Apple in 1998, became COO in 2005, ran day-to-day operations during Jobs's medical leaves in 2004, 2009 and early 2011, and was named CEO on 24 August 2011, the day Jobs resigned. In August 2018 Apple became the first US public company valued at USD 1 trillion. Disney: Bob Chapek succeeded Bob Iger in February 2020; the board replaced Chapek and brought Iger back in November 2022.

- **Works councils** in Germany and the Netherlands must be consulted on many organisational changes (stated generally; no thresholds).
- **Pakistan:** a certified union acts as the collective bargaining agent (Industrial Relations Act 2012 and provincial IR Acts). This is the only "Pakistan" mention left; it is deliberate, as one example among several jurisdictions.
- **ISO 37002 (2021)**: guidance on whistleblowing management systems.
- **EU Whistleblower Directive (2019/1937)**: internal reporting channels for "many employers" (deliberately not stating the 50-worker threshold) and a ban on retaliation.
- **US Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002)** protects employees of listed companies who report fraud (s.806); **UK Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998** protects workers making qualifying disclosures.
- **ISO 45003 (2021)**: guidance on managing psychosocial risks at work; "many countries' health and safety laws cover work-related stress" (general).
- Unchanged from v2 and worth a glance: WHO describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition (ICD-11); model attributions (Kotter, Prosci ADKAR, Heifetz, Cynefin/Snowden, Maister trust equation, Edmondson, Cohen and Bradford, Cialdini, Schein, Cressey).

## Agentic AI for Business (`agentic-ai`)

Please verify the facts below before publication. Everything is paraphrased; figures are rounded.

#### Real cases used

##### Session 1: The Launch Bay
1. **Mata v. Avianca (US, 2023)**, example card "Real case: the invented court citations".
   - Lawyers for a passenger suing Avianca in federal court in New York filed a brief that cited about six court decisions that did not exist; they had been generated by ChatGPT.
   - The lawyer asked ChatGPT whether the cases were real and it said they were.
   - June 2023: the judge (S.D.N.Y.) sanctioned two lawyers and their firm with a USD 5,000 fine. He said using AI was not improper in itself; the failure was not checking and standing by the false citations after they were questioned.
2. **Knight Capital (US, 2012)**, example card "Real case: 45 minutes without an off switch".
   - 1 August 2012: a deployment left old, unused code active on one of eight servers, which sent a flood of unintended orders.
   - About 45 minutes to stop; loss of about USD 460 million (often reported as about USD 440m); emergency funding, then a takeover agreed within months (merger with Getco, KCG).
   - 2013: the SEC fined it USD 12 million, finding inadequate controls to prevent erroneous orders and respond to problems (market access rule).

##### Session 2: The Engine Deck
3. **Moffatt v. Air Canada (Canada, 2024)**, example card "Real case: the chatbot an airline could not disown".
   - 2022: the website chatbot told a traveller a bereavement fare could be claimed after travel, within 90 days; the airline's policy page (linked by the chatbot) said otherwise.
   - February 2024: British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal found negligent misrepresentation, rejected the argument that the chatbot was a separate entity responsible for its own actions, and ordered payment of about CAD 800 (about CAD 650 damages plus interest and fees; total about CAD 812).
4. **Morgan Stanley Wealth Management assistant (US, 2023)**, example card "Real case: an adviser assistant built on retrieval".
   - September 2023 rollout to financial advisers of an assistant built on OpenAI's GPT-4, answering from the firm's library of around 100,000 research reports and documents.
   - Statement to verify: "both the firm and OpenAI have described expert grading of answers as central to the build" (based on OpenAI's published customer story on Morgan Stanley's evaluation approach).

##### Session 3: The Chart Room
5. **Klarna AI assistant (Sweden, 2024)**, example card "Real case: Klarna's AI assistant, by the numbers".
   - Company press release, February 2024, first month: about 2.3 million conversations; two-thirds of service chats; work equivalent to about 700 full-time agents; resolution in under 2 minutes versus 11; satisfaction on a par with humans; about 25% fewer repeat enquiries; estimated USD 40 million profit improvement in 2024.
   - Statement to verify: "In 2025 its chief executive said publicly that customers would always be able to reach a human, and the company put more weight on service quality" (widely reported in May 2025).
6. **Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, "Generative AI at Work" (NBER working paper, 2023)**, stats diagram "Real case: AI help in a support centre".
   - 5,179 customer-support agents at a large software company; about 14% more issues resolved per hour on average; about 34% for novice and less-skilled workers; little gain for the most experienced and skilled.
7. **McDonald's and IBM automated order taking (US, 2024)**, example card "Real case: a drive-through pilot that was stopped".
   - Test of AI voice ordering with IBM from 2021; June 2024 the company told franchisees it would end the test (in more than 100 restaurants) by late July 2024 and would keep evaluating voice ordering options.
   - Social media videos of misheard orders drew wide attention during the test (stated as attention, not as a cause).

##### Session 4: The Shield Array
8. **Samsung Electronics and ChatGPT (South Korea, 2023)**, example card "Real case: source code pasted into a chatbot".
   - Early 2023, soon after staff in the semiconductor business were allowed to use ChatGPT, employees were reported to have entered confidential source code and internal meeting notes.
   - May 2023: Samsung told staff it was temporarily restricting generative AI tools on company devices and internal networks while it put security measures in place and worked on internal tools.
9. **Dutch childcare benefits scandal (Netherlands, 2020–2021)**, example card "Real case: a risk model that brought down a government".
   - Thousands of parents wrongly treated as fraudsters and asked to repay large sums.
   - Parliamentary inquiry report, December 2020, titled "Unprecedented Injustice" (Ongekend onrecht); the Dutch government resigned in January 2021.
   - December 2021: the Dutch data protection authority (Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens) fined the tax administration EUR 2.75 million for unlawful, discriminatory processing of applicants' dual nationality, including the use of nationality as an indicator in a risk-classification model.

##### Session 5: The Fleet Horizon
10. **DPD chatbot (UK, 2024)**, example card "Real case: the parcel chatbot that turned on its owner".
    - January 2024: a customer prompted the chatbot to swear and to write a poem criticising DPD; his screenshots went viral.
    - DPD said an error had occurred in the AI element after a system update, disabled it immediately and said it was being updated.
11. **Commonwealth Bank of Australia voice bot (Australia, 2025)**, example card "Real case: a bank reverses AI job cuts".
    - July 2025: 45 customer-service roles to go after the introduction of an AI voice bot.
    - The Finance Sector Union challenged the decision at the Fair Work Commission, arguing call volumes were rising.
    - August 2025: the bank reversed the decision and apologised, saying its initial assessment had not adequately considered all relevant business considerations; affected staff offered to stay, redeploy or leave.

#### Regulatory statements to confirm (session 4, "Which frameworks reach your agents?" and "Same principles, different rulebooks")
- OECD AI Principles adopted 2019, updated 2024.
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (January 2023), functions Govern, Map, Measure, Manage.
- ISO/IEC 42001:2023, AI management system standard (certifiable).
- GDPR: limits on decisions based solely on automated processing with legal or similarly significant effects (Article 22); widely used as a model by other laws.
- EU AI Act: in force August 2024, obligations phased in from 2025; creditworthiness assessment of individuals and recruitment/employment uses listed as high-risk. Course deliberately gives no application dates for high-risk duties, as these have been subject to proposed changes.
- UK: no single AI law; regulators such as the FCA and ICO apply existing rules, including UK data protection law.
- Singapore: MAS FEAT principles (2018) for financial firms; national Model AI Governance Framework.
- UAE: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 on personal data protection; separate data protection regimes in DIFC and ADGM.
- India: Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. Kenya: Data Protection Act, 2019.
- Pakistan: national AI policy approved in 2025; central-bank (SBP) technology-risk and outsourcing rules apply; personal data protection bill pending (learners told to check status).
- US: no comprehensive federal AI law; sector regulators and a growing number of state laws.
- General statement: banking regulators in most jurisdictions treat an external model provider as an outsourcing/third-party and cloud arrangement (due diligence, data location, security, exit). Quiz ai-s4-q8 relies on this.

## AML/CFT Compliance Professional (`aml-cft`)

All figures are rounded in the course. Please verify each fact below against the official sources named.

#### Real cases used

##### Session 1
1. **1MDB** (example card "Real case: 1MDB, from bonds to a superyacht"; keypoints)
   - Malaysian state investment fund set up in 2009.
   - US DOJ: more than USD 4.5bn misappropriated, 2009 to 2015 (DOJ asset-forfeiture filings).
   - Funds moved through companies with names that looked like real partners, then shell companies and accounts in several countries;
     spent on luxury homes in New York and Los Angeles, a private jet, a superyacht, artworks and the financing of a Hollywood film (*The Wolf of Wall Street*, not named in the course).
   - 2016: MAS (Singapore) shut down BSI Bank's Singapore operation.
   - 2020: Goldman Sachs global resolution, more than USD 2.9bn; its Malaysian subsidiary pleaded guilty in the US.
   - 2020: Malaysia's former prime minister convicted in the SRC International case (a former 1MDB unit), upheld on appeal in 2022. He is not named in the course.
2. **FATF grey list** (timeline "Real cases: the grey list in action"; quiz s1-q4; exam x3, x8)
   - FATF founded 1989 by the G7; special recommendations on TF added October 2001; 40 Recommendations revised 2012, including TFS for PF (R.7).
   - Pakistan: grey-listed June 2018 (TF-focused action plan); second, ML-focused plan added 2021; removed October 2022 after an on-site visit.
   - UAE: grey-listed March 2022; removed February 2024 after its action plan was judged substantially complete and an on-site visit.
   - The UAE detail "FATF asks the UAE to show results, for example in ML prosecutions and supervision of risky sectors" is a paraphrase of the action plan's themes. Please confirm.

##### Session 2
3. **TD Bank** (example card; exam x16 explanation)
   - October 2024: TD Bank's US arm pleaded guilty to Bank Secrecy Act programme failures (conspiracy). Total about USD 3bn (DOJ, FinCEN, OCC, Federal Reserve).
   - OCC imposed an asset cap on its US bank subsidiaries.
   - DOJ: about 92% of transaction volume (about USD 18.3 trillion) not monitored, over roughly a decade (2014 to 2023).
   - "Flat cost paradigm" for compliance spending.
   - Three laundering networks moved more than USD 670m, some by bribing bank employees.
4. **Commonwealth Bank of Australia, intelligent deposit machines** (example card; quiz s2-q5; s4 stats)
   - IDMs rolled out from 2012. ML/TF risk not assessed until mid-2015.
   - About 53,500 late threshold transaction reports (cash of AUD 10,000 or more), caused by a coding error. The course says "more than 53,000".
   - Late or missing suspicious matter reports, including activity by drug syndicates.
   - 2018: AUD 700m civil penalty, at the time the largest in Australian corporate history.

##### Session 3
5. **Danske Bank, Estonian branch** (example card; keypoints)
   - About EUR 200bn of payments through the non-resident portfolio, 2007 to 2015, per the Bruun & Hjejle report the bank commissioned and published in September 2018.
   - Many customers were shell companies registered abroad. Owners and source of funds were often not understood. Warnings from a whistleblower and correspondent banks were not acted on quickly.
   - The group CEO resigned in 2018.
   - December 2022: Danske pleaded guilty in the US to conspiracy to commit bank fraud (misleading US correspondent banks about its controls) and agreed to forfeit about USD 2bn.
6. **Panama Papers** (stats diagram)
   - April 2016, ICIJ and partners; 11.5 million documents from Mossack Fonseca, covering 1977 to 2015; more than 214,000 offshore entities.
   - Iceland's prime minister resigned in April 2016. Mossack Fonseca announced its closure in March 2018.
   - ICIJ (2019): more than USD 1.2bn recovered in taxes and penalties worldwide.

##### Session 4
7. **NatWest and Fowler Oldfield** (example card; stats)
   - Bradford jeweller. About GBP 365m deposited 2012 to 2016, of which about GBP 264m was cash. Expected turnover at onboarding was much lower.
   - Cash deposited at branches around the country, at one point in bin bags. A system error meant some cash was not treated as cash by monitoring.
   - December 2021: guilty plea to three offences under the Money Laundering Regulations 2007, the first criminal prosecution of a bank under them (FCA). Fine about GBP 264.8m.
8. **Westpac** (stats): AUD 1.3bn penalty approved by the Federal Court in 2020; over 23 million contraventions; about 19.5 million international funds transfer instructions not reported; weak monitoring for child-exploitation risk patterns (AUSTRAC).
9. **HSBC** (stats): 2012 US deferred prosecution agreement, about USD 1.9bn; at least USD 881m of drug-trafficking proceeds (Sinaloa and Norte del Valle cartels) laundered through HSBC Mexico and HSBC USA.
10. CBA is repeated in the s4 stats card (see item 4).

##### Session 5
11. **BNP Paribas** (example card)
    - 2014: guilty plea in the US. About USD 8.9bn for processing US-dollar transactions for Sudanese, Iranian and Cuban parties, mainly 2004 to 2012.
    - Payments routed through other banks with references to sanctioned parties removed, so US filters would not stop them. Compliance warnings not acted on.
    - One-year suspension of some US-dollar clearing (NYDFS).
12. **Binance** (example card; quiz s5-q10)
    - November 2023: guilty plea (failure to maintain an effective AML programme, unlicensed money transmitting, IEEPA sanctions violations). About USD 4.3bn.
    - Founder and CEO pleaded guilty to failing to maintain an effective AML programme and stepped down.
    - FinCEN: failed to report more than 100,000 suspicious transactions, including some linked to terrorist groups and ransomware. Trades processed between US users and users in sanctioned jurisdictions such as Iran. Many users could trade without full KYC.

#### Regulatory statements to confirm
- **Country examples** (s1 compare card; quiz s1-q6, s1-q8; exam x5, x6):
  - US: Bank Secrecy Act; FinCEN is the FIU.
  - UK: POCA 2002 and Money Laundering Regulations 2017; SARs go to the UK FIU in the NCA; the FCA supervises.
  - EU: AML directives plus the 2024 AML package; national FIUs; AMLA in Frankfurt coordinates supervisors and will directly supervise some firms.
  - UAE: a federal AML/CFT decree-law, "first issued in 2018 and since updated". The course gives no number because a newer decree-law may have replaced No. 20 of 2018. Also: UAE FIU via goAML; the Central Bank supervises.
  - Singapore: CDSA; STRO is the FIU; MAS supervises.
  - Pakistan: AMLA 2010 and ATA 1997; the FMU is the FIU; SBP and SECP supervise.
- **Cash thresholds:** US CTR at USD 10,000 (structuring to evade it is itself a crime); Australian threshold transaction report at AUD 10,000. No Malaysian threshold is stated; the Kuala Lumpur story avoids naming one on purpose.
- **Record keeping:** FATF minimum of 5 years. The course calls 5 years the basic period in the EU and UK. Pakistan is given as an example of a longer period, shown as "10 years" (carried over from the previous version; please confirm against the current AMLA).
- **PEPs:** the UK and the EU apply enhanced measures to domestic PEPs too (UK MLR reg. 35; the 2024 UK amendment makes lower risk the starting point for domestic PEPs, which the course does not mention).
- **Beneficial ownership:** questions assume "a 25% threshold". The course says only that the threshold is set in current regulations.

## Communication Skills for Professionals (`communication-skills`)

Course file: `content/courses/communication-skills.json`. The story world is fictional: **Kestrel Foods**, which has a
regional hub in Dubai and a plant in Rotterdam, launches **Lumo**, a chilled yoghurt drink, in London, Dubai and Singapore.
Real organisation names appear only in the "Real case" cards listed below. All case text is in my own words, and the
figures are rounded. Please check each fact against a primary source before publication.

#### Real cases used, with the facts relied on

##### Session 1: Signals and Static
1. **Tenerife runway collision, 1977** (example card, plus a check card)
   - 27 March 1977, Los Rodeos airport, Tenerife. Two Boeing 747s (KLM and Pan Am) had been diverted there and the airport was in fog.
   - The KLM aircraft began its take-off run while the Pan Am aircraft was still taxiing on the same runway. 583 people died, making it the deadliest accident in aviation history.
   - The KLM co-pilot used a non-standard phrase, roughly "we are now at take-off". The tower replied "OK" and then told KLM to stand by. Those words were lost because Pan Am transmitted at the same moment, which blocked both messages.
   - Afterwards aviation tightened standard phraseology ("take-off" is used only when a take-off is cleared or cancelled; "departure" is used otherwise) and placed more weight on read-backs. *Please confirm the wording of the ICAO phraseology change.*
2. **Avianca Flight 52, 1990** (example card)
   - 25 January 1990. A Boeing 707 flying from Colombia to New York JFK was held in long holding patterns in bad weather, ran out of fuel and crashed at Cove Neck, Long Island. 73 of the 158 people on board died.
   - The crew said they were low on fuel and asked for "priority", but did not declare an "emergency".
   - NTSB probable cause, paraphrased: the flight crew failed to manage the fuel load adequately and failed to communicate an emergency fuel situation to ATC before the fuel ran out.

##### Session 2: Writing That Gets Read
3. **US "off-channel communications" penalties, 2022** (example card)
   - 27 September 2022. The SEC charged 16 firms (15 broker-dealers and one affiliated investment adviser), with penalties of about USD 1.1bn. On the same day the CFTC announced about USD 710m against 11 firms. That makes **about USD 1.8bn in total**.
   - Staff used personal devices, text messages and apps such as WhatsApp for business communications, and the firms did not preserve those messages as record-keeping rules require. Further cases followed in 2023–2024.
4. **Mars Climate Orbiter, 1999** (example card)
   - The spacecraft was lost on 23 September 1999 during Mars orbit insertion: it came too close to the planet.
   - NASA's Mishap Investigation Board found that the root cause was a failure to use metric units in a ground software file. Thruster impulse data was produced in pound-force seconds where newton-seconds were expected. The board also cited inadequate communication between project teams and verification that failed to catch the error.
   - The cost is described only as "worth well over USD 100m". Commonly cited figures are about USD 125m for the orbiter and USD 327.6m for the wider Mars Surveyor '98 mission. *Please confirm before publishing.*

##### Session 3: Presenting With Confidence
5. **Apple iPhone launch keynote, 2007** (example card)
   - 9 January 2007, Macworld, San Francisco. Steve Jobs announced "three revolutionary products": a widescreen iPod with touch controls, a revolutionary mobile phone and a breakthrough internet communications device. He repeated the three, then revealed that they were one device.
   - The claims that his slides were mostly one image or a few words, and that the keynote is widely studied, are general characterisations.
6. **Columbia briefing slides, 2003** (example card, plus a check card)
   - During the January 2003 launch, foam struck Columbia's left wing. The shuttle broke up on re-entry on 1 February 2003 and all seven astronauts died.
   - The Columbia Accident Investigation Board (report, August 2003) concluded that the foam strike breached the left wing's leading edge. It criticised NASA's reliance on PowerPoint briefing slides in place of technical papers.
   - On a Boeing debris-analysis slide, the title suggested the test data showed conservatism. The key caveat, that the test projectiles were far smaller than the foam that hit Columbia, appeared low on the slide in small type, several bullet levels down. (The board drew on Edward Tufte's analysis of the slide.)

##### Session 4: Meetings, Feedback and Difficult Conversations
7. **Amazon narrative memos, from 2004** (example card)
   - In June 2004 Jeff Bezos sent an e-mail ending PowerPoint presentations at Amazon's senior team ("S-team") meetings and replacing them with narrative memos.
   - In his 2017 letter to shareholders he described six-page memos. Meetings begin with silent reading of the memo.
8. **Google Project Aristotle, 2015** (example card, plus a check card)
   - Google's People Analytics researchers studied 180+ Google teams. In 2015 they reported, on Google's re:Work site, that how a team works together matters more than who is on it.
   - Psychological safety came first, followed by dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, and impact.
9. **United Airlines Flight 173, Portland, 1978** (example card)
   - 28 December 1978. A DC-8 had a landing-gear indication problem. The crew circled for about an hour, ran out of fuel and crashed in a suburb of Portland, Oregon. Ten people died (eight passengers and two crew).
   - NTSB probable cause, paraphrased: the captain failed to monitor the fuel state and to respond properly to it and to the crew's advisories. A contributing factor was that the other crew members either did not fully grasp how critical the fuel state was or did not successfully communicate their concern to the captain.
   - United went on to create one of the first crew resource management (CRM) programmes (around 1981). CRM is now standard in aviation and widely used in healthcare.

##### Session 5: Influence Across Cultures, Screens and Levels
10. **Tylenol recall, 1982** (example card)
    - Autumn 1982. Seven people in the Chicago area died after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules laced with cyanide. The tampering is believed to have happened after the product left the factory.
    - Johnson & Johnson warned the public, stopped advertising and recalled about 31 million bottles nationwide, at a cost widely reported as about USD 100m. It relaunched the product in tamper-resistant packaging.
    - The card says only that the brand "went on to win back much of its market". It makes no specific market-share claim and does not mention the unsolved criminal case.
11. **"Zoom fatigue" research, 2021** (example card)
    - Jeremy N. Bailenson (Stanford), "Nonverbal Overload: A Theoretical Argument for the Causes of Zoom Fatigue", *Technology, Mind, and Behavior*, February 2021.
    - The paper proposes four causes: excessive close-up eye contact, seeing yourself constantly, reduced mobility, and higher cognitive load in sending and reading non-verbal cues. Its suggestions include hiding the self-view, using a smaller window and taking audio-only breaks.
12. **Challenger launch decision, 1986** (example card)
    - On the eve of the launch, Morton Thiokol engineers raised concerns about O-ring performance in cold temperatures. In the teleconference with NASA, Thiokol management reversed the engineers' recommendation not to launch.
    - On 28 January 1986 Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after lift-off and all seven crew died.
    - The Rogers Commission found that an O-ring seal failure in the right solid rocket booster caused the disaster. It also found a flawed decision-making process: Thiokol's concerns were not communicated to the top-level NASA managers who approved the launch.

   - CrowdStrike's review: a bug in its content validator let the update through; it was deployed to all customers at once; commitments to staged roll-outs and more customer control.
8. **TSB IT migration, April 2018** (card "Real case: TSB's migration weekend (2018)")
   - Customer accounts migrated from the former parent's (Lloyds Banking Group) systems to a new platform over one weekend; widespread login, data and payment problems lasting months.
   - Independent review commissioned by TSB (Slaughter and May, 2019) found the platform not ready and testing inadequate.
   - December 2022: FCA and PRA fines totalling about GBP 48.65 million (shown as "about GBP 49 million"), for failures in managing operational risk of the migration and its IT supplier.

##### s5 Disaster recovery and security
9. **Maersk and NotPetya, June 2017** (card "Real case: Maersk rebuilds after NotPetya (2017)")
   - NotPetya spread via a compromised update of Ukrainian accounting software (M.E.Doc).
   - Maersk chairman (Jim Hagemann Snabe, Davos 2018) said about 4,000 servers and 45,000 PCs were reinstalled in roughly ten days; ports fell back on manual work.
   - Maersk estimated the cost at USD 200–300 million (2017 interim report).
   - The only surviving copy of Active Directory was on a domain controller in Ghana that was offline during a power cut (widely reported, e.g. Wired 2018).
10. **Colonial Pipeline ransomware, May 2021** (card "Real case: an old password shuts a pipeline (2021)")
   - Pipeline shut for about five days; fuel shortages and panic buying in several states.
   - CEO testimony to the US Senate (June 2021): entry via a legacy VPN account no longer in use, password only, no MFA.
   - Ransom about USD 4.4 million; US DOJ recovered about USD 2.3 million.

## Procurement Management (`procurement-management`)

Story world is fictional: Lumina Foods (Dubai hub; plants in Malaysia, Egypt and a new one near Nairobi), film supplier Jadeline Flexpack (Jebel Ali), a second film maker in Istanbul, Kamau Freight and all bidders. All story money is in USD and illustrative.

#### Real cases used (facts relied on)

##### s1
- **Commission v Netherlands (UNIX), Court of Justice, 1995** (Case C-359/93). A Dutch public contracting authority required computer systems to run on UNIX without "or equivalent". The Court held this breached EU public supply rules on technical specifications. Used for: brand/standard names in specs, "or equivalent".
- **Aisin Seiki fire, Japan, February 1997.** Fire at an Aisin plant that made almost all of Toyota's P-valves (brake proportioning valves). Toyota's Japanese assembly stopped for several days. Many group and non-group suppliers (dozens) produced valves from Aisin's drawings, and production was largely back to normal within about a week. Used for: bottleneck items, single-source risk.
- **Kraljic matrix origin:** Peter Kraljic, Harvard Business Review, 1983 (diagram caption).

##### s2
- **InterCity West Coast franchise, UK, 2012.** DfT announced FirstGroup as winner; Virgin Rail sought judicial review; in October 2012 the government cancelled the competition, citing significant technical flaws in how the DfT ran it (including how risk and subordinated loan/guarantee amounts were calculated). Laidlaw inquiry and NAO reported. The course says the cancellation "cost taxpayers tens of millions of pounds" (NAO put it at at least about GBP 50m; please confirm wording).
- **NDA Magnox decommissioning tender, UK.** High Court (Fraser J), July 2016, EnergySolutions EU Ltd v Nuclear Decommissioning Authority: evaluation found to be wrong and scores manipulated/"fudged"; correct evaluation would have put the claimant consortium first. In 2017 the NDA settled for nearly GBP 100m (about GBP 85m plus costs, about GBP 97m total); the contract was later terminated early and an independent inquiry (Holliday) was ordered. Course states "several billion pounds" contract value and "nearly GBP 100 million".

##### s3
- **UK PPE "VIP / high-priority lane", 2020-2022.** NAO (November 2020) reported that suppliers in the high-priority lane were much more likely to win contracts (course says only "far more likely"). High Court, 12 January 2022 (Good Law Project & EveryDoctor v SSHSC, O'Farrell J): operation of the lane breached the obligation of equal treatment and was unlawful; the two contracts examined (PestFix, Ayanda) would probably have been awarded anyway.
- **Los Angeles LED streetlight programme, 2009-2013.** More than 140,000 streetlights replaced with LEDs; city reported energy use for those lights down by more than 60% (reported figure about 63%) and savings of millions of dollars a year. **Please verify the rounded figures.**

##### s4
- **Tesco v Unilever ("Marmitegate"), October 2016.** After the post-referendum fall in sterling, Unilever sought price rises from UK retailers (reported around 10%); Tesco refused and Unilever products including Marmite were briefly unavailable on Tesco's online store; resolved within about a day; terms not disclosed.
- **Ever Given, Suez Canal, 23-29 March 2021.** Grounded 23 March, refloated after six days; hundreds of ships queued; some rerouted around Africa; owner declared general average.
- **Cavendish Square Holding v Makdessi / ParkingEye v Beavis, UK Supreme Court, 4 November 2015.** Penalty test: whether the clause imposes a detriment out of all proportion to the innocent party's legitimate interest.

##### s5
- **Rimasauskas / fake Quanta Computer invoices, 2013-2015.** Lithuanian national impersonated Quanta Computer; Google and Facebook paid more than USD 100m (reported about USD 121m); extradited to the US, pleaded guilty to wire fraud in 2019, sentenced in December 2019 to five years.
- **Philips Albuquerque fire, March 2000.** Lightning-triggered fire at a Philips chip plant in New Mexico; fire out in minutes but clean rooms contaminated. Nokia reacted quickly and secured alternative supply; Ericsson, single-sourced for key chips, reported losses of hundreds of millions of dollars linked to the shortage; in 2001 Ericsson moved handsets into the Sony Ericsson joint venture.
- **Stats card "the cost of looking away":**
  - Rana Plaza collapse near Dhaka, Bangladesh, 24 April 2013: more than 1,100 deaths (1,134 commonly cited); building housed garment factories supplying global brands.
  - South Africa construction cartel, June 2013: Competition Commission fast-track settlement, 15 firms, about ZAR 1.46bn in penalties, bid rigging including 2010 World Cup stadium projects.
  - Siemens, December 2008: about USD 1.6bn in total paid to US (DOJ/SEC, USD 800m) and German (Munich) authorities over bribery. Course wording: "about the total paid to US and German authorities to settle bribery charges linked to winning contracts". **Confirm the total framing** (some sources count the 2007 German fine separately).
  - Fake Quanta invoices: USD 100m+ (as above).

## Talent Management (`talent-management`)

#### Real cases, by session

##### s1: Workforce planning and selection
1. **Google drops brainteasers (2013).** Card "Real case: Google drops the brainteasers (2013)".
   - In a 2013 New York Times interview (Adam Bryant, June 2013), Laszlo Bock, then Google's SVP of People Operations,
     said Google's own analysis found brainteasers did not predict job performance and mainly made interviewers feel clever.
   - Google moved towards structured interviews (consistent behavioural/situational questions, shared scoring guides);
     Bock described this in *Work Rules!* (2015).
2. **Amazon's experimental CV-scoring tool (reported 2018).** Card "Real case: Amazon's experimental CV-scoring tool (2018)".
   - Reuters (Jeffrey Dastin, October 2018) reported Amazon built an experimental tool from about 2014 that rated CVs
     one to five stars, trained on about ten years of past applications, mostly from men.
   - It penalised CVs containing the word "women's" (example: "women's chess club captain").
   - Engineers edited it to neutralise those terms but could not be sure of other discrimination; the project was dropped.
   - Amazon said recruiters never used the tool's rankings alone to evaluate candidates.

##### s2: Onboarding and performance management
3. **Wells Fargo sales-practices scandal (2016, 2020).** Card "Real case: Wells Fargo's sales targets (2016)".
   - September 2016: combined penalties of about USD 185m (CFPB USD 100m, OCC USD 35m, Los Angeles City Attorney USD 50m).
   - Regulators found about 2 million deposit and credit-card accounts that may not have been authorised (about 1.5m deposit, about 565,000 card).
   - Bank said it had fired about 5,300 employees over several years for related misconduct.
   - February 2020: USD 3bn settlement with the US Department of Justice and SEC (criminal and civil).
4. **Microsoft ends stack ranking (2013).** Card "Real case: Microsoft drops its rating curve (2013)".

   - November 2013: HR head Lisa Brummel's internal memo ended the curve and numerical ratings, with emphasis on feedback, teamwork and growth.
   - Also mentioned (caption of the performance-cycle diagram): **Adobe** replaced annual reviews with ongoing "Check-ins" in 2012.

##### s3: Learning and development
5. **AT&T reskilling, "Workforce 2020" (from about 2013).** Card "Real case: AT&T reskills its workforce (2013 onwards)".

   - Widely reported estimate: about 100,000 employees in roles that might not exist within a decade (e.g. NYT 2016; HBR 2016).
   - Online courses and degrees with partners including Udacity and Georgia Tech (online MS in Computer Science); career portal showing growing/shrinking roles.
   - Investment reported at about USD 1bn over several years. **Please confirm the figures above are the commonly cited ones.**
6. **Singapore SkillsFuture (2015).** Card "Real case: Singapore's SkillsFuture (2015)".
   - SkillsFuture launched 2015; from January 2016 every Singapore citizen aged 25+ received an opening SkillsFuture Credit of SGD 500; later top-ups for some groups; employer training subsidies exist.

##### s4: Careers and succession

##### s5: Retention, rewards, inclusion, analytics
9. **Google Project Oxygen (2008 onwards).** Card "Real case: Google's Project Oxygen (2008 onwards)".
   - People analytics project begun 2008; used performance reviews, feedback surveys and manager-award nominations.
   - Found better-rated managers had better-performing teams with lower turnover; identified eight behaviours (good coach; empowers without micromanaging; interest in team members' success and wellbeing; results-oriented; good communicator; helps with career development; clear vision; key technical skills, ranked last).
   - Upward feedback surveys and manager training built around them; reported by the New York Times (Adam Bryant, March 2011) and HBR (Garvin, 2013).
10. **Salesforce pay audits (2015 onwards).** Card "Real case: Salesforce's pay audits (2015 onwards)".
    - 2015: after two senior women executives (Cindy Robbins, Leyla Seka) raised concerns, Marc Benioff approved a company-wide pay review.
    - First adjustments about USD 3m, affecting about 6% of employees, men and women; repeated in later years, partly because acquisitions brought different pay structures.
11. **Uber workplace review (2017, 2019).** Card "Real case: Uber's 2017 workplace review".
    - February 2017: former engineer Susan Fowler's blog post described harassment by a manager and HR inaction on complaints, including from other women.
    - Uber commissioned Eric Holder (Covington & Burling) to review culture and practices; June 2017 the board adopted the recommendations (47 in total).
    - CEO Travis Kalanick resigned in June 2017.
    - December 2019: USD 4.4m settlement with the US EEOC over sexual harassment and retaliation.
